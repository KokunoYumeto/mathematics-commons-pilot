# Target-language selection

Choose one exact language and record its locale, script, and orthographic standard. A broad label such as “Chinese,” “Arabic,” or “Serbo-Croatian” is insufficient when the intended edition depends on region, script, or standard.

The separate older-manuscript archive records rows in Arabic, German, English, Spanish, Persian, French, Indonesian, Interslavic, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Sanskrit, Ukrainian, Vietnamese, multiple Chinese conventions, and several mixed or undetermined records. Those aggregate tags do not describe the open-education suggestions in this starter. Check the selected work's own edition records before treating a language as covered. Missing data means unknown, not absent.

Translations into any language are welcome. Give priority to languages for which communities document limited access to university-level mathematical material, and record the intended learners and available reviewers. A 2025 [UNESCO report announcement](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/new-unesco-report-calls-multilingual-education-unlock-learning-and-inclusion) says that 40% of people lack access to education in a language they speak and understand fluently, more than a quarter-billion learners are affected, and only 351 of roughly 7,000 spoken or signed languages are used as a medium of instruction.

No official UNESCO list of 96 underserved languages was identified. The often-repeated 96 figure appears to confuse a different UNESCO statistic: roughly 3% of the world's population speaks 96% of its languages. Do not publish a numbered language-priority list under UNESCO's name without an exact source. A Commons-maintained priority list is acceptable if it publishes its criteria and per-language evidence.

A valid target may be any language not already maintained for the selected work. Declared independent overlap is also allowed when it has a clear review or edition purpose.
